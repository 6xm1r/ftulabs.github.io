Building a V100 SXM2 GPU Cluster on a University Budget

2026-06-11 · FTU Labs · 10 min read

Our 40-node Docker Swarm cluster runs LLM inference on 88 commodity laptops — great for serving small models (4B–8B) at scale, but fundamentally limited by integrated GPUs and 16 GB of RAM per node. When we started working with 28B–35B parameter models, we needed real GPU compute. This post covers the hardware side of building our first dedicated GPU node: why we picked each component, how we put it together, and what the numbers look like.

Part 2 will cover the system design — Docker integration, model serving stack, and how this node fits into the existing Swarm.

Why V100 SXM2

The V100 was NVIDIA's flagship datacenter GPU from 2017–2020. In 2026 it's two generations behind (Ampere, Hopper have since shipped), which is exactly why it makes sense for us. Used V100-SXM2-16GB modules go for a fraction of what even a single consumer RTX 4090 costs, yet they offer something no consumer card does: NVLink.

The V100 comes in two form factors, and the difference matters:

  • V100 PCIe — standard PCIe x16 card. Plugs into any server motherboard. Limited to PCIe 3.0 bandwidth (~15.75 GB/s per direction) for GPU-to-GPU communication.
  • V100 SXM2 — mezzanine module that sits directly on a baseboard. Connected via NVLink NV2: 6 links per GPU, ~300 GB/s aggregate bisection bandwidth. That's roughly 10× the bandwidth of PCIe.

We went with SXM2 because our workload — serving a 28B-parameter model across 4 GPUs — needs the GPUs to exchange KV cache and activation data constantly. With PCIe, inter-GPU communication becomes the bottleneck. With NVLink, the 4 GPUs behave more like a single 64 GB device.

Our 4×V100 SXM2 node provides:

  • 64 GB total HBM2 VRAM (4 × 16 GB)
  • NVLink NV2 full-mesh topology — every GPU pair has ~51.5 GB/s bidirectional bandwidth
  • Volta architecture (sm_70), 5120 CUDA cores per GPU
  • 640 Tensor Cores per GPU (FP16/FP32 mixed-precision)

The main trade-off: Volta (sm_70) does not support Flash Attention 2, which requires Ampere (sm_80) or newer. This rules out some software stacks but is not a dealbreaker — llama.cpp and vLLM both have alternative attention backends that work on sm_70.

The Baseboard

SXM2 GPUs don't plug into standard motherboard slots. They mount on a purpose-built baseboard — a custom PCB that provides NVLink traces, power delivery, and the mezzanine connectors. The baseboard we sourced is an 8-GPU design (we populated 4 of the 8 slots), which gives us room to scale later.

The baseboard connects to the host system via a PLX PCIe switch, which bridges the SXM2 modules to a standard PCIe x16 slot on the host motherboard. From the OS perspective, you see 4 CUDA devices on a single PCIe root — but the real inter-GPU traffic flows over NVLink, not PCIe.

Each GPU module comes with a copper heatsink pre-mounted. These are passive heatsinks — no fans, no vapor chamber, just solid copper fin stacks. In a datacenter chassis (like the DGX-1) they rely on high-velocity airflow from chassis fans. On our open bench, we added 120mm blower fans at each end of the board for directed airflow. Under sustained load, GPU temps sit around 75–80°C — well within the 83°C throttle threshold.

Why EVGA 2000W

Each V100 SXM2 module draws up to 300W TDP. Four GPUs alone can pull 1200W at peak. Add the host system (CPU, RAM, NVMe, fans) and you're looking at ~1400W total. We needed a PSU with enough headroom to handle sustained loads without tripping protections.

The EVGA SuperNOVA 2000 G+ was the choice for several reasons:

  • 2000W continuous on a single unit — no need to synchronize dual PSUs. Dual-PSU setups are common in server builds but introduce complexity (you need a splitter board or jumper to sync the start signals, and if one PSU trips, the other keeps running, which can damage components).
  • 80+ Gold efficiency — at 50% load (our typical operating point of ~1000W), it runs at ~92% efficiency, meaning ~80W wasted as heat from the PSU itself.
  • Enough PCIe power connectors — the baseboard needs multiple 8-pin (6+2) EPS/PCIe power inputs. The EVGA 2000 G+ ships with enough cables to supply them all natively, without splitters or adapters.
  • Single 220V input — Vietnam's grid runs at 220V/50Hz, so 2000W is achievable on a standard 15A outlet. On US 120V circuits, you'd need a 20A dedicated circuit or a 240V outlet for this wattage.

Host System

The GPU baseboard needs a host system for the CPU, boot drive, and network connection. We kept this minimal — the host's job is to orchestrate, not compute:

  • CPU: Intel i7-13700 (16 cores, 24 threads) — enough to feed 4 GPUs without bottlenecking on tokenization or scheduling
  • RAM: 14 GB DDR4 — tight, but sufficient for our inference workload where the model lives entirely in VRAM
  • Storage: 476 GB NVMe (boot + models) + 175 GB secondary NTFS partition for overflow
  • OS: Ubuntu 26.04 LTS, CUDA 13.0, driver 580.159.03
  • Network: Gigabit Ethernet on the campus LAN (10.0.110.x subnet) + Tailscale VPN for remote access

The storage situation is the weakest link. A quantized 28B model (GGUF Q4_K_M) is ~18 GB — that eats a significant chunk of a 476 GB drive. We work around this by NFS-mounting the model directory from the Swarm's leader node, so all nodes in the cluster share a single model cache.

The Build Process

Assembling an SXM2 system is not like building a regular PC. There's no case, no standard standoffs, no I/O shield. The baseboard sits on a cardboard box (its shipping container) while we work on it — it's open-bench by necessity.

The build went through a few stages:

  1. Inspecting the baseboard — we received the board with 4 V100 modules pre-seated. First step was checking each module's seating and verifying the NVLink connector alignment. A multimeter helped us verify power rail continuity before connecting the PSU.
  2. Wiring the PSU — the baseboard uses a combination of 8-pin EPS connectors for GPU power and a 24-pin ATX connector for board logic. Each GPU pair needs its own dedicated power cable — daisy-chaining is a fire hazard at 300W per module.
  3. Connecting to the host — a PCIe x16 riser cable bridges the baseboard's PLX switch to the host motherboard. Keep it short — long risers introduce signal integrity issues.
  4. First power-on — the whole team gathered for this one. We connected a monitor and keyboard, hit the power button, and watched nvidia-smi enumerate 4 GPUs. Seeing all 4 V100s recognized with NVLink active was the moment the project felt real.
  5. Thermal management — without a server chassis, we attached 120mm blower fans with zip ties and tested under sustained GPU burn-in. The copper heatsinks do their job if you provide even modest airflow.

Initial Benchmarks

With the hardware assembled, we ran our first inference benchmarks using llama.cpp with our REAP-28B model (a pruned 28B-parameter Qwen variant quantized to Q4_K_M, ~18 GB GGUF).

A key constraint: mainline llama.cpp's draft-model speculative decoding loads the model twice (once as target, once as draft), which OOMs on 2×16 GB. With 4×V100 (64 GB total) and the shared-model memory mapping from the AtomicChat fork, the model loads once and all 4 GPUs share the mapped memory.

Hardware No Speculative +MTP (draft d=2) Acceptance Rate
V100 4×SXM2 78 t/s 61 t/s 15%
H100 (1×) 141 t/s 157 t/s 63.6%
Xavier AGX 18.9 t/s 10–12 t/s 15%
i7-13700 (CPU only) 25.5 t/s 38.2 t/s ~49%

The V100 node hits 78 tokens/second on single-stream generation — roughly 4× faster than CPU inference and 4× faster than the Xavier AGX. For context, that's fast enough for real-time conversational AI (human reading speed is ~4 tokens/second).

An interesting finding: speculative decoding (MTP) is net-negative on Volta. The V100 is compute-bound, not memory-bandwidth-bound, so the overhead of draft-then-verify exceeds the savings from parallel token generation. MTP only breaks even at ~60% acceptance rate — our V100 achieves 15%, far below the threshold. On the H100 with our improved KD-MEGA MTP head (63.6% acceptance), speculative decoding does deliver a +12% speedup.

Cluster-wide Context

To put this node in perspective against the rest of our infrastructure:

Tier Hardware Nodes Model Per-Node Speed
Swarm i7-13700H laptops 40 Qwen 4B Q4_K_M ~8–10 t/s
Edge Jetson Xavier AGX 1 REAP-28B Q2_K ~15–19 t/s
GPU 4×V100 SXM2 1 REAP-28B Q4_K_M ~78 t/s

The Swarm handles volume (40 nodes, ~396 t/s aggregate at 80 concurrent users) with small models. The V100 node handles quality — a single node running a 28B model at conversational speed. The semantic router in front of the cluster directs simple queries to the Swarm and complex reasoning tasks to the V100 node.

Lessons and What's Next

A few things we learned building this:

  • SXM2 boards are not plug-and-play. Unlike PCIe GPUs, there's no standard ecosystem of cases, cables, and cooling. You're essentially building custom hardware. Budget time for debugging power delivery and thermals.
  • NVLink matters more than clock speed for multi-GPU inference. The bandwidth between GPUs determines how fast you can serve models that don't fit in a single GPU's VRAM. PCIe would cut our effective throughput significantly.
  • Don't skimp on the PSU. At 1200W+ GPU load, a marginal PSU will trip under sustained inference. Get a unit rated for continuous (not peak) delivery at your target wattage. The EVGA 2000W has been rock-solid through multi-day burn-in runs.
  • sm_70 is still capable — it can't run Flash Attention 2, but alternative attention backends (standard SDPA, custom V100-optimized kernels) get within 80% of FA2 performance for inference workloads.
  • Speculative decoding isn't universal. Test on your actual hardware before committing to an MTP-based serving strategy. Compute-bound GPUs (V100, Xavier) don't benefit; bandwidth-bound GPUs (H100) do.
